Why should I appeal?

Very few denials are appealed, and about a third of appeals win.

How we got these numbers: Why should I appeal?

Independent review in California

On HealthCare.gov plans, the federal marketplace, insurers reversed 35% of the internal appeals they reported in 2024 (85,560 of 247,951). Independent review is the step after an insurer's own appeal, when outside doctors who don't work for the insurer decide. We show California because its Department of Managed Health Care publishes every independent review decision it oversees, case by case. There, in 2025, outside doctors overturned 72% of the denials they reviewed, up from 47% in 2017. These are cases people chose to take that far, and that qualified, so the rate isn't the chance that any denial would be reversed.

Prior authorization denials

Insurers now post how often they deny prior authorization, and the spread is wide. Prior authorization is when a plan has to approve care before you get it. Since March 2026, Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid plans, state Medicaid programs and most HealthCare.gov insurers have had to post what share of 2025 requests for medical care and equipment they denied. Prescription drugs aren't included. Insurers count differently, so compare within one kind of plan.

Not every plan. Big insurers post one report per contract or state, and these 89 are the largest we found. AuthDenied has a much fuller collection, more than 1,100 plans, and KFF compares the largest insurers.

Your plan's clock

Starting in 2026, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid and Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP) plans must answer a prior authorization request by these deadlines. A state-run CHIP program may follow its state's law instead.

  • Urgent requests: 72 hours.
  • Other requests: 7 days.
  • More time: sometimes up to 14 more days, for either kind.
  • A missed deadline: in Medicare Advantage and Medicaid plans, it counts as a denial you can appeal.

A denial must tell your doctor the specific reason. HealthCare.gov insurers must give the reason too, but they still have 15 days (72 hours if urgent). These rules don't cover prescription drugs, job-based plans, state-run marketplaces (even those that sign people up through HealthCare.gov) or Original Medicare.

From CMS's fact sheet on the rule and the federal regulations for each kind of plan, checked October 23, 2026.

Denials over time

More denials, in network and out. HealthCare.gov plans, by the year the claims were made. In-network denials about doubled from 2020 to 2024, to 84.5 million, as enrollment grew. Insurers have reported out-of-network claims only since 2022, and denied 37% of them in 2024, about twice the in-network rate.

Who doesn't have coverage?

About 27 million people under 65 in 2024, about 1 in 10, from 3.3% in Massachusetts to 19.1% in Texas.

Uninsured rates for people under 65 are Census estimates for 2024, and the health measures are CDC estimates for adults, mostly from 2023. States with higher uninsured rates also tend to have more adults in fair or poor health. The link is weaker for diabetes and absent for depression. This compares states, not people, and doesn't show that one causes the other.

Where the money goes: prescription drugs in Medicare

How we got these numbers: Drug costs

A Medicare price tag marks a drug whose price Medicare negotiated with the drugmaker, or picked for a price that starts in 2028. That price caps what Medicare drug plans pay for the drug, plus a pharmacy fee, and it's not your copay: your plan sets your share, based on that price, so ask your plan how it affects you. Xarelto's ends after 2026 because a generic is now sold. Medicare's list of negotiated drugs.

Medicare Part D spending, before rebates

Help with Medicare drug costs

  • A yearly cap. In a Medicare drug plan, or a Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage, the most you pay for covered drugs is $2,100 in 2026 and $2,400 in 2027. After that you pay nothing for covered drugs for the rest of the year. Discount cards used instead of your plan don't count toward it. How drug costs work.
  • Extra Help. If your income and savings are limited, Medicare's Extra Help helps pay drug plan premiums, deductibles and copays. In 2027 its copays are at most $5.80 for a generic and $14.40 for a brand-name drug. See if you qualify for Extra Help.
  • Monthly payments. The Medicare Prescription Payment Plan spreads what you owe for drugs across the year. It's free, but it doesn't lower your costs, and Medicare says it may not be the best choice if you join after September, so think about it for 2027. To join, contact your plan. How the payment plan works.
  • Free advice. Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) gives free one-on-one Medicare counseling, including on drug plans. Find yours at shiphelp.org or by calling 1-877-839-2675.

What you can do

A denial is a first answer, not a final one

1. Find out why

Ask your insurer for the specific reason and the documents they used. Employer, marketplace and Medicaid plans have to give you the claim file for free. Medicare Advantage and Medicare drug plans have to give it to you too, but can charge for copying and mailing it. If the reason is paperwork or coding, a corrected claim may fix it without an appeal.

2. Appeal inside the plan

Employer and marketplace plans give you at least 180 days. Medicare Advantage gives 65 days from the date on the notice, and Medicaid plans give 60. Your denial letter has your deadline. In 2024, HealthCare.gov insurers reversed about a third of the internal appeals people filed, and there was about 1 appeal for every 320 in-network denials.

3. Ask for independent review

If the plan says no again, most denials about medical judgment can go to outside doctors, through your state or the federal process. In California, independent reviewers sided with patients in 72% of the cases they decided in 2025.

4. Get help writing it

Fight Health Insurance drafts an appeal from your denial letter for free, with the medical evidence and the rules that apply to your plan. Not sure what your denial means? Get it explained.

Who to call

Start your appeal with the steps in your denial letter. For questions or complaints, these offices can help, by kind of coverage.

  • A job-based plan from a private employer (not a government or church plan): the U.S. Labor Department's benefits advisors, at askebsa.dol.gov or 1-866-444-3272.
  • Medicare, including Medicare Advantage and drug plans: 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227, TTY 1-877-486-2048), or Medicare's complaint form for problems with a plan. To fight a denial, appeal with the steps in your notice.
  • A plan you bought yourself, or a job-based plan your employer buys from an insurer: your state's insurance department. Find it in this directory of state insurance departments.
  • A marketplace decision about whether you can buy a plan or get savings (or how much, or a special enrollment period): appeal to the marketplace itself, generally within 90 days of your eligibility notice, on HealthCare.gov's appeals page or your state marketplace's site. A denial from your insurer is a different appeal, the kind the rest of this page and Fight Health Insurance help with.
  • A surprise medical bill: the No Surprises Help Desk, 1-800-985-3059, or CMS's medical bill rights page.
  • Free Medicare counseling: your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP). Find yours at shiphelp.org or by calling 1-877-839-2675.
  • Your state: our state help pages list each state's insurance department, Medicaid office, SHIP and independent review.

What this can't show yet

The biggest plans report the least

Most Americans get coverage through an employer. The Affordable Care Act calls for those plans to report denials too, but federal regulators have never put that into effect. A few states, like Connecticut, publish figures for the plans they regulate, which leaves out employers' self-funded plans. That gap is part of the story.

Employer plans

About half the country. Federal law calls for denial reporting, but regulators have never put it into effect.

Medicaid and Medicare Advantage

No claim-denial data by state. The prior-authorization reports above cover requests made before care, a different step from claims.

Daily numbers

No public source reports denials more often than once a year. The counter above spreads a yearly total evenly; it isn't a live feed.

Procedure prices

Hospital price files are public but messy, and the standard procedure names are licensed. A future cost explorer would use plain-language names.

Sources and method

How we got the numbers in each section, and the choices we made.

The denial and appeal records

  1. Denials and appeals: CMS Transparency in Coverage public use files, plan years 2022 to 2026, which report claims from 2020 to 2024. Individual-market medical plans only, in states using HealthCare.gov. Insurers report these figures to CMS themselves. Numbers are FHI's totals of issuer-level figures, not CMS's own, and closely match KFF's 2024 analysis (KFF leaves out insurers with 1,000 or fewer claims, so it counts 39 fewer appeals). The headline figures count in-network claims, as KFF does: 84.5 million denials in 2024, 19% of in-network claims, and 263,021 internal appeals, about 1 for every 320 of those denials. The trend chart can switch to out-of-network claims or all claims, and the map to all claims; the insurer table counts in-network claims only. Cells CMS suppresses for small counts are left out.

Why should I appeal?

  1. Source: the denial and appeal records above.
  2. Appeals: the counts aren't split by network, so the 1-in-320 figure compares two totals, as KFF's does. Appeal win rates count only insurers that reported both appeals filed and appeals won, and an appeal won in part counts as won. Nine insurers' 2024 appeals-won counts look like reporting errors, so we leave them out too. Four reported none of their 346 to 1,813 appeals won, after winning a quarter to nearly half the year before. For three, CMS marked the win rate as suppressed but printed the counts anyway. Two reported exactly the same counts as in 2023. Their appeals still count as filed. With them, the 2024 rate would be 33.6%. We show only the latest year's win rate, because the insurers reporting change from year to year.
  3. Independent medical review: California DMHC IMR determinations, via the CalHHS Open Data Portal, 2010 to 2025 (file as published June 2026), with exact duplicate records removed. That's the step after an insurer's own appeal. Its rate isn't directly comparable with the insurers' own appeal results: different plans, people and review processes.

What are they denying, and why?

  1. Source: the denial and appeal records above.
  2. In network and out: CMS's data dictionary defines the one claims count in the files for 2020 and 2021 as in-network only, so the other two views start in 2022. In 2024 insurers denied 16.4 million of 44.4 million out-of-network claims (37%) and 100.8 million of 494.7 million claims overall (20%), the same national rates KFF reports. For 8.6 million of those out-of-network denials, about half, insurers gave the reason that the plan doesn't cover care outside its network (CMS's closed-network reason, reported per plan; one claim can count under more than one reason). KFF doesn't break out-of-network rates down by state or insurer, and we don't map them by state: in 2024, 120 of the 157 insurers reporting them sold only HMO or EPO plans (from CMS's 2024 plan list), which mostly don't cover out-of-network care by design, so a state's rate would mostly show which kinds of plans its insurers sell, not how they handle claims. We don't show them insurer by insurer either: even among insurers that also sold PPO or POS plans, many had most of their out-of-network claims on HMO or EPO plans, or gave that closed-network reason for most of those denials. The insurer table has no all-claims rates for the same reason: they fold in those out-of-network denials, so an insurer whose plans mostly don't cover out-of-network care could rank worse for that alone. Two insurers reported more out-of-network denials than claims in 2024, which can't be right: Moda Health Plan in Alaska and Blue Cross Blue Shield of North Dakota. We leave them out of the out-of-network and all-claims figures, and the map's all-claims view shows no figure for Alaska or North Dakota. Blue Cross and Blue Shield of Kansas (2022 claims) and Blue Cross Blue Shield of North Dakota (2023) are left out of those years the same way. There's no out-of-network change from 2023, which mostly reflects which insurers reported, and no out-of-network appeals figure. The states and insurers that report change each year (Georgia and Illinois have since left HealthCare.gov, and insurers that leave stop reporting), and rates aren't adjusted for the kinds of care people used.
