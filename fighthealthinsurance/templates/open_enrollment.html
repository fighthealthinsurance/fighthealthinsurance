What to do now

    {% if enrollment %}
  • See plans and prices first. HealthCare.gov shows plans and estimated prices for your area, based on your income, before you log in.
  • Update your income and household. When your marketplace's enrollment opens, put your expected {{ enrollment.plan_year }} income and household on your application, so you get the right savings.
  • Compare plans and costs on HealthCare.gov or your state's marketplace{% if enrollment.medicare %}, or in Medicare Plan Finder for Medicare{% endif %}, even if you'll be renewed automatically.
  • Check your doctors and prescriptions. Plans change every year, so look for yours in each plan's doctor and drug lists.
  • Weigh insurers' denial rates. See how often your state's insurers denied claims, where we have figures (mostly 2024), and how often large plans denied prior authorization: one thing to weigh, not a quality rating, and some of those insurers don't sell plans for {{ enrollment.plan_year }}.
    • {% else %}
  • Report income and household changes. If either changes, update your application as soon as you can, so your savings stay right.
    • {% endif %}
  • Pay the first premium. A plan you pick only starts once the first month's premium is paid, if there is one.
  • Disagree with a marketplace decision? If it says you can't buy a plan or get savings, you can appeal to the marketplace, generally within 90 days of the notice.
  • Get free help. Find an independent assister near you or call 1-800-318-2596.

Signing up for coverage

Enrollment questions

Already have a marketplace plan?

During open enrollment, log in, update your income and compare plans, even if you'll be renewed automatically. Plans and prices change every year, and a new plan only starts once you pay the first premium, if it has one. For free, unbiased help, find an independent assister near you or call 1-800-318-2596. Our fall checklist has the rest.

Lost coverage, or about to?

Losing health coverage can open a special enrollment period, usually from 60 days before it ends to 60 days after (90 days after, for Medicaid or CHIP), and you may need to send proof that you lost it. Medicaid and CHIP take applications any time of year. See what counts on HealthCare.gov, or find free help near you.

{% if enrollment %}

When can I sign up for a {{ enrollment.plan_year }} HealthCare.gov plan?

CMS has set HealthCare.gov's {{ enrollment.plan_year }} sign-up dates, but the court ruling behind them is under appeal, so check HealthCare.gov's dates before you count on a deadline. State-run marketplaces may have different dates. Oregon now has its own marketplace: for {{ enrollment.plan_year }} plans, Oregon residents sign up at ExploreHealthOR.gov.

{% endif %}

Is picking a plan the same as having coverage?

No. A plan picked in open enrollment only becomes coverage once the first month's premium is paid, if there is one. CMS counts everyone with a plan picked, including people renewed into one automatically. The number with coverage in effect in February was 90% as many as picked a plan for 2025, and 83% as many for 2026.

What does CMS's count include?

Everyone with a HealthCare.gov or state-run marketplace plan in effect in February, nationwide. February is the latest month CMS has published for 2026, so every year is compared on that month. The 2026 count isn't final yet: CMS keeps updating a year's numbers until its December data are in.

How does CMS's count compare with KFF's?

KFF, a nonprofit health policy research, polling, and news organization, counts people who buy their own health plan, on the marketplace or off it, from insurers' filings, and compares June with June:

Enrollment in the individual health insurance market fell 18% between June 2025 and June 2026.

From KFF's analysis of October 14, 2026. It counts a wider group in a later month than CMS's count, so the two numbers aren't meant to match.

Does leaving the marketplace mean going uninsured?

Not always. In a KFF survey of adults who had a marketplace plan in 2025, taken in February and March 2026, 22% said they had moved to other coverage, such as through a job, Medicare, Medicaid or a plan bought outside the marketplace, and 9% said they were uninsured. KFF notes that some plans sold outside the marketplace may cover less and have fewer consumer protections. The Census uninsured figures on Denial Watch are for 2024, before the 2026 drop in marketplace coverage.

Do Denial Watch's denial rates cover the same plans?

Partly. The denial rates on Denial Watch's state map are for HealthCare.gov insurers' marketplace plans, from 2024 claims. Its every-state chart and insurer table add five states that run their own marketplace, from other sources or years: California and Pennsylvania from their insurers' own reports for 2024, which can include plans sold outside the marketplace, and Georgia, Illinois and Virginia from earlier years. CMS's enrollment count covers every marketplace nationwide.

Sources and method

Open enrollment and marketplace coverage

  1. Marketplace coverage: CMS Health Insurance Exchanges Monthly Effectuated Enrollment, the file with data as of May 5, 2026 (Data_asof_20260505), which runs to February 2026. Coverage in effect is what CMS calls effectuated enrollment: an active plan, with its first month's premium paid if it has one. These are national totals for February of each year, HealthCare.gov and state-run marketplaces together, all from this one file. CMS updates each month's count until the year's December data are in, so 2026 is preliminary and may change. Earlier CMS reports counted the same month sooner: its early 2025 report had 23.4 million for February 2025 (counted March 15, 2025), against 21.8 million in this file, so mixing reports would make the drop look bigger. CMS notes that Minnesota's latest data leave out a subgroup of enrollments after a reporting change; without Minnesota, the 2026 drop is 11.9%. CMS revised its 2021 counts for a new method (its early 2022 report), and its notes for this file don't say whether 2016 to 2020 were counted again the same way.
  2. Plan picks: CMS's Marketplace Open Enrollment Period public use files for 2025 and 2026, the national total from the state-level file, HealthCare.gov and state-run marketplaces together. CMS counts people, not plans, including people renewed into a plan automatically. In CMS's words, "plan selections will only become coverage for consumers that effectuate their coverage by paying their first monthly premium" (public use files FAQs). The 90% and 83% divide each February's coverage by that year's plan picks. CMS's early 2025 report gave 96%, from its earlier February count.
    3. {% include "partials/coverage_mix_method.html" %}
  3. Plans picked, 2014 to 2026: For 2017 to 2026, the national total and its HealthCare.gov and state-run parts from CMS's Marketplace Open Enrollment Period state-level public use files, one for each year; 2025 and 2026 are the plan picks above. CMS has no file covering every marketplace before 2017, so 2014 to 2016 are from HHS ASPE's enrollment reports for 2014, 2015 and 2016, whose tables cite CMS data. Each count is of people with a plan picked, including people renewed automatically, not paid coverage, and leaves out Basic Health Program plans. The 2014 window ran from October 1, 2013 to March 31, 2014, and its count includes sign-ups through April 19, 2014. HealthCare.gov's deadline was December 15 for 2018 to 2021 and January 15 for 2022 to 2026 (January 16 for 2024); state-run marketplaces set their own deadlines. So the sign-up window was about six months for 2014, about six weeks on HealthCare.gov for 2018 to 2021, and about eleven weeks there for 2022 to 2026. For HealthCare.gov, CMS counted a picked plan for 2017 if its coverage included March and wasn't canceled, for 2018 to 2021 if any of its coverage wasn't canceled, and for 2022 on only if its coverage ran to at least January 31 (the 2017 file's definitions and CMS's 2022 public use files FAQs). CMS doesn't check state-run marketplaces' figures, and the HealthCare.gov and state-run parts shift when a state changes platform, as Georgia did for 2025 and Illinois for 2026. Checked October 25, 2026.
  4. Marketplace coverage and people under 65: People under 65 are the U.S. Census Bureau's population estimates by single year of age for July 1 of the year before each February: its 2010 to 2020 intercensal estimates (nc-est2020int-agesex-res.csv) for 2015 to 2019, and its Vintage 2025 estimates (nc-est2025-agesex-res.csv) for 2020 to 2025. Where the two join, in April 2020, the newer series counts 0.07% more people under 65. Census revises every year from 2020 on with each new set of estimates; the next estimates by age are due in April 2027. No one publishes a yearly count of everyone who could buy a marketplace plan, so the chart compares coverage with everyone under 65. CMS's count is for all ages: about 1.7% of 2025's plan picks were by people 65 or older. It leaves out Basic Health Program plans, such as New York's Essential Plan; CMS's 2025 open enrollment file counts about 1.8 million people in them. February 2026 is CMS's first count for that month and may change. Checked October 26, 2026.
  5. KFF: Matt McGough, Justin Lo and Cynthia Cox, "Individual Market Enrollment Continues to Fall Through Mid-2026" (KFF, October 14, 2026): plans people buy themselves, on and off the marketplaces, 25.2 million in June 2025 and 20.5 million in June 2026, from insurers' filings compiled by Mark Farrah Associates. The survey is "Cost Concerns and Coverage Changes: A Follow-Up Survey of ACA Marketplace Enrollees" (KFF, March 19, 2026): 1,117 adults who had marketplace plans in 2025, asked from February 12 to March 2, 2026, with a margin of error of plus or minus 3.8 points.
